Abstract: The use of supervised deep neural networks (DNN) for automatic modulation classification (AMC) in the military and civilian sectors, such as spectrum monitoring, specific radiation source identification, and cognitive radio, has received widespread attention. However, this method faces challenges such as generalization errors and false correlations. To capture and extract more abstract and useful features to enhance downstream task performance, "Contrastive Learning," a promising representation learning method, has achieved significant success in computer vision and natural language processing. Its core is to learn effective features by maximizing the similarity between different views of the same data sample in the latent space.
This paper proposes a contrastive objective function based on Contrastive Learning aimed at improving the transfer performance of models on low SNR datasets. Compared with denoising methods used for noise signal classification in the past, this method abandons the constraint of pairwise inputs, allowing the model to utilize any combination of noisy signals and clean signals within the same category. Additionally, an estimation module for noise level is introduced to enhance the model's robustness in uncertain noise environments. 2. Introduction Automodulation classification (AMC) refers to the recognition of the modulation pattern of received signals, which is a key technology in spectrum management, cognitive electronic warfare, and various civilian applications. For example, in large-scale wireless communication signal reconnaissance and detection, AMC can be used to determine the operating parameters of enemy electronic transmitters, providing support for subsequent combat strategy formulation. However, in practical applications, AMC faces numerous challenges: on one hand, traditional maximum likelihood criteria and feature-based methods have high computational complexity and sometimes cannot obtain the optimal solution; on the other hand, when there is insufficient data, severe noise or interference pollution, the implementation difficulty of AMC will significantly increase.
In recent years, supervised deep neural networks (DNNs) have been considered as potential solutions for efficiently solving AMC problems. This end-to-end paradigm allows direct input of raw data, automatically learning internal representations required for different tasks without the need for manual feature design, thus learning complex mapping relationships in high-dimensional data compared to traditional techniques. For example, Ramjee et al. proposed an optimized CLDNN model by integrating DenseNet and LSTM architectures, achieving classification accuracy around 90% on a dataset containing 10 modulation types when the signal-to-noise ratio is greater than 2dB; Li et al. designed the MCNet model using asymmetric convolutional kernels, residual connections, and multiscale feature maps, achieving classification accuracy of 93.59% on the RadioML 2018.01A dataset (containing 24 modulation types) with a signal-to-noise ratio of 20dB; Dong et al. proposed the semi-supervised learning framework SSRCNN, which achieves approximately 94% stable average accuracy on the RadioML 2016.04C dataset through a multi-component loss function consisting of cross-entropy loss, KL divergence loss, and center loss combined with both labeled and unlabeled data training, showing stronger interference resistance against noise pollution.
However, current classifiers generally lack distribution-outside generalization ability, and their performance significantly drops when the training and test data are displaced due to random noise or other external factors. There are few research results in the field of AMC addressing this issue. The previously proposed denoising classification network (DNCNet) can improve the classification accuracy of models trained on high signal-to-noise ratio datasets on noisy data, but it relies on paired clean and noisy data, which is difficult and time-consuming to obtain clean training samples.
Currently, the industry generally believes that the paradigm of pretraining with unlabeled data and fine-tuning on supervised downstream tasks can provide models with better initial parameters and find a more generalized optimal solution. The pretext task in the pretraining phase can learn effective representations without labeling, mainly including three types: firstly, reconstruction tasks (such as MAE, GPT, BERT), which achieve by removing part of input data and training the model to predict the missing parts; secondly, comparison tasks (such as MoCo, BYOL, SimCLR), which maximize the similarity between different views of the same data in latent space to learn features; thirdly, generation tasks (such as GAN, BiGAN, BigGAN), which model the probability distribution of data through adversarial losses between generator and discriminator to mine semantic information.
This paper focuses on the automatic modulation classification problem, aiming to enhance the robustness of deep models in the face of signals with different noise types and intensities. The main contributions are as follows:
1  
Drawing on the learnable nonlinear transformations and momentum update mechanisms between different view representations in SimCLR and MoCo, we design a contrastive learning deep model suitable for AMC tasks in noisy environments. This model randomly selects data from noise and clean signals of the same category at different sampling times, without relying on paired noise-clean training data (avoiding the limitation of generating noise data by adding random noise to clean signals). It meets the actual receiving scenario requirements and can adapt to various types of noise.
2  
A noise level representation module is proposed, which concatenates with clean and noisy signals to input into the encoder of a contrastive learning framework. This module can extract discriminative features from the input data, serving as an indicator signal for noise level, thereby enhancing the robustness of the model in handling signals with varying signal-to-noise ratios. It avoids the significant drop in accuracy of traditional DNN classifiers when the signal-to-noise ratio decreases, allowing the model to converge to a more stable optimal solution.
3  
Comprehensive performance comparison between the proposed method and existing advanced methods is conducted on both public communication signal dataset and synthetic radar signal dataset. In addition to the commonly used additive Gaussian white noise, the method's effectiveness was also verified on colored Gaussian noise and pulse noise signals.

Network Model Introduction The proposed Comparative Self-Supervised Learning Autonomous Modulation Classification Architecture (CSSL-AMC) follows the "Unsupervised Pretraining - Supervised Fine-Tuning" paradigm, where the core is to compare input multi-view through contrastive learning to determine whether the input pairs belong to the same category (positive sample pairs) or different categories (negative sample pairs), and to push positive sample pairs closer and push negative sample pairs further by a similarity function. The model treats clean and noisy signals as input multi-views, divided into two stages of learning:
1  
Pretraining Phase (Pretext Task): Extracting universal features of unlabeled signals through contrastive loss. Noisy signals of the same category are input to a noise level estimator (an enhanced view of noisy samples as clean samples), and the output noise intensity indicator signals are concatenated with the original signals before being input into the encoder. The contrastive loss is minimized through backpropagation, measuring the similarity between clean and noisy signals in the latent space.
2  
Post-fine-tuning phase (downstream task): Transfer the pre-trained representations to the downstream classification task, and update network parameters with labeled data. Compared with the random initialization training, the supervised fine-tuning strategy has higher classification accuracy and better generalization performance on low signal-to-noise ratio noisy data.
(I) In non-pairwise input designs (such as radar signal interception), influenced by transmission path loss and human interference, the receiver obtains signals with varying signal-to-noise ratios within the same sampling period, making it impossible to simultaneously acquire high and low signal-to-noise ratio pairwise signal data. Therefore, non-paired inputs are more in line with practical application needs.
This design is based on the difference in the deterministic distribution of modulation signals and the random distribution of noise: non-pairwise input data selected under the same environment follows the same probability distribution, representing signal instances at different time points. Specifically, data randomly selected from the same category of noise samples (noise_samples) and clean samples (clean_samples) form non-pairwise inputs (as shown in Figure 6). Compared to pairwise inputs (clean signals at the same time point with noise signals), the noise signals in non-pairwise inputs come from different time points. Since each signal sample has a dimension of 2ÃN (the length of the signal sequence), it is necessary to first shuffle the clean and noise signal datasets randomly, then randomly select data to construct non-pairwise inputs with a size of "batch_size ×2×N".
From the perspective of feature learning effectiveness, in pairwise inputs, the encoder processes and separately extracts clean signal distributions related to random noise from individual samples. In contrast, non-pairwise inputs train the encoder through the same category and \(\tilde{x}_j(i\) \), allowing it to process more intra-class samples and noise in a single iteration, thereby achieving a deeper understanding of the intra-class distribution.
(II) Noise Level Representation Module: When the signal-to-noise ratio of the signal data differs significantly from that of the training data, the performance decline in DNN is attributed to noise pollution and the model's lack of robustness/generalization ability for unknown datasets. To avoid designing separate models for different noise levels of data, this paper adopts a Fully Convolutional Network (FCN) as the noise level indicator module.
FCN includes 3 convolutional layers without pooling operations, with output channels of 32, 32, and 2 respectively. The stride is set to 3 and zero-padding is employed to ensure the consistency of input and output data dimensions. The FCN output is then concatenated with the signal data in channel dimension before being input into subsequent DNN (as shown in Figure 7(b)). The feature vectors output by FCN can represent noise intensity, providing auxiliary information for processing data with different signal-to-noise ratios.
Compared to the noise level plots constructed based on the standard deviation of noise in [46], this FCN module can handle signal data with unknown signal-to-noise ratio (SNR); at the same time, it avoids the difficulty introduced by using the noise level plot as a regularization term. When inputting non-paired signal data of the same category but different noise strengths (e.g. 30dB and 5dB SNR), the classifier needs to classify them into the same category. In this case, the FCN can capture discriminative features beyond the signal itself, enhancing the model's robustness in handling unseen SNR signal data.

Conclusion: To address the issue of out-of-distribution sample overfitting caused by noise pollution in deep neural network-based automatic modulation classification (AMC), this paper proposes a signal detection and surveillance method based on Deep Neural Networks (CSSL-AMC). This method learns the feature correlation between clean and noisy samples by pulling together their representations in the embedding space and pushing apart those of different classes, eliminating the dependence on paired clean-noisy input data that previous denoising methods have.
To enhance the model's generalization capability across a wide range of signal data with varying signal-to-noise ratios, a noise level estimator is designed during the feature representation process.
